How to choose what to play at game night

Updated 17 November 2026 · 3 min read

In short

Filter first, then vote. Start from the games that fit the number of people coming and the time you have, ask each person for one or two suggestions, and let everyone vote for every game they would happily play instead of just one favorite. Settle a tie with the shorter game, or let whoever teaches it decide.

Choosing the game can take longer than scheduling the night. The person with the biggest collection lists twenty titles, the newcomer has heard of none of them, and the loudest voice wins. A short, fair process saves the time and gives everyone a say.

1. Start from the player count

The number on the box is the range a game supports, not the range where it plays best. For a second opinion, look the game up on BoardGameGeek, where players vote on the best and recommended player counts. Keep only the games that work well for the number of people who are actually coming.

2. Fit the time and the energy

A weeknight after work suits something that plays in 60 to 90 minutes. A long weekend afternoon can take a heavy strategy game. Count the rules explanation too: a new, complex game can spend half an hour on the teach before anyone takes a turn.

3. Think about the newest player

If someone is new to modern board games, pick something with a short teach and simple turns, so they spend the evening playing instead of catching up. Cooperative games are another good option for mixed groups, because the whole table works through the rules together.

4. Let everyone suggest, within limits

Ask each person for one or two suggestions. That keeps the list short enough to vote on, gives quieter players a say, and stops one collection from taking over the evening. Whoever owns a game should confirm they can bring it and are happy to teach it.

5. Vote for everything you would enjoy

When each person can vote for only one game, the votes split between favorites and someone ends up playing a game they dislike. Let everyone vote for every suggestion they would be glad to play. This is called approval voting: the winner is the game the most people are happy with, which matters more on game night than the game one person loves most.

6. Break ties fairly

  • Play the shorter game first, and the other one if there is time.
  • Let the person who will teach the game decide.
  • Give the pick to whoever chose least recently.

7. Take turns picking in a regular group

Groups that meet often can skip the vote now and then and take turns choosing. It spreads the teaching load, brings new games to the table, and gives everyone a night that feels like theirs.
